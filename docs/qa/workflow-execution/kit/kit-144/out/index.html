Which RUV designs can MultiScholaR offer, and which one should be the default?

Simulation study of unwanted-variation removal across four omics · final results as scored on 18 November 2026 · simulations complete in all four omics, with a check on public real data

MultiScholaR removes unwanted variation (batch, run order, sample handling) with RUV before testing for differences between groups. We tested every way the package could do this on simulated data where the truth is known, and then checked the main result on public real data. One design keeps false discoveries close to the 5% target in every omic at almost no cost in power, and it held on real data in all four omics. The design the package uses today does not: it returned 30% to 56% false discoveries in its worst main scenario, and too many false calls on real data in all four omics.

Two things to know before reading on. First, the recommended design did not pass every one of the study's own pre-set rules in every omic; we chose it as the default because it was the best-calibrated design we measured, and its measured limits are listed below. Second, one result on real data disagrees with the simulations (not about the recommended design); it is stated plainly below.

  • The recommended design matches the best possible result in three of four omics, and stays inside the limit in the fourth.   • Today's design finds the same number of real differences but reports many false ones. It is worst when the unwanted factor partly follows the groups, and in DIA and lipidomics/metabolomics it is liberal in nearly every scenario.
  • Not correcting is not a safe fallback. It keeps the error rate in three omics only by finding almost nothing (power under 1% in transcriptomics and lipidomics/metabolomics when unwanted variation is present).

Does it hold on real data?

Yes for the recommended design, in all four omics. Today's design made too many false calls on real data in all four. One simulation result was not confirmed on real data: not correcting at all was fine in the real DIA data set, although the simulation said it would not be.

The test: take one public data set per omic, pick 12 samples from a single condition and split them into two made-up groups of 6. Nothing truly differs between the made-up groups, so anything the analysis reports is false. This was repeated 100 times per data set. Two limits were written down before the runs: on average at most 7.5% of features with p below 0.05 (5% is the target), and not many more splits with a reported difference than without RUV (at most twice as many, or at most 10 of 100).

  • The one disagreement with the simulations. In the DIA simulation, not correcting gave too many false positives when the unwanted factor partly followed the groups (11.6% against a 7.5% limit). On the real DIA data set, with the real batch lined up with the made-up groups in the same way, not correcting gave 3.7% and no split with a false call. By the rule we wrote beforehand this counts as a contradiction. We measured one possible reason: the batch effect in that real data set is about a quarter to a third as strong as the unwanted factor we simulated. That fits, but it is one data set and it does not prove the reason.
  • What this means. The simulations are a hard test: they may overstate how much harm skipping RUV does when unwanted variation is weak. The disagreement does not touch the recommended design, which was within its limits on real data as in the simulations.
  • What the real-data check does not show. It counts false calls only. It cannot show that a design finds more real differences; that evidence is from the simulations. Only three designs were run on real data (recommended, today's, none), on one data set per omic.

When should we not trust the recommended design?

It is safest in classic proteomics and transcriptomics. In DIA, be careful with 3 samples per group and with several unwanted factors at once. In lipidomics and metabolomics, be careful when batch strongly follows the groups or when a known batch is unbalanced.

These come from a second tier of deliberately hard simulated scenarios (13 or 14 per omic, 500 data sets each). A scenario counts as "outside a limit" when the design reports clearly too many false discoveries, has too many bad single data sets, or gains nothing over no RUV. Scenarios in which even the best possible analysis fails are set aside, because they cannot tell us anything about a design.

  • Batch strongly following the groups is hard for everything, and worst for not correcting: without RUV, false discoveries were 45% to 64% in that scenario in every omic. The recommended design gave 5.2% to 13.0%.
  • Several unwanted factors or very few samples strain the automatic count of factors. It found all three factors in classic proteomics and transcriptomics at 6 per group, but too few in DIA and at 3 per group.
  • Lipidomics and metabolomics with 3 per group could not be assessed at all: even the best possible analysis failed in those scenarios.
  • Today's design was outside a limit in almost every hard scenario in classic proteomics, DIA and lipidomics/metabolomics (false discoveries up to 68%), and in 5 of 11 in transcriptomics (up to 72%).

Why this was run

A review of the package found that its RUV step, although each computation matches the published software exactly, is used differently from the method papers in three ways. Each could plausibly inflate false discoveries, and code inspection cannot say by how much.

ChoiceToday's designMethod papers
Negative-control featuresThe least significant 5 to 30% of features from a first test, picked by a grid searchReplicate-based RUV: all features "works well in practice" (RUVSeq). Control-only RUV: keep most features, drop only the clearly changed ones
Number of unwanted factors (k)Chosen by the same grid searchNo fixed rule; judgment with diagnostics. RUVSeq's example uses 1
What the test readsClassic proteomics, lipidomics, metabolomics: the corrected dataRNA-seq and limma convention: uncorrected data with the unwanted factors in the model; corrected data "only for exploration"

The package will be described in a methods paper and shipped to users who will not check these choices themselves. The study was run to put a measured error rate beside every option the software offers, and to choose the default on evidence.

What was run

SimulatorsFour, one per analysis route: classic proteomics; DIA proteomics with limpa; transcriptomics (counts); lipidomics and metabolomics (two assays, internal standards, pooled QC injections). Each generates data with known true differences and a known unwanted factor, then runs the package's own code path.
Main scenariosEight per simulator. A centre scenario (6 samples per group, 20% of features changed, one unwanted factor unrelated to the groups), and seven that each change one setting: 12 per group; 10% changed; no unwanted factor; no true differences at all; unwanted factor partly following the groups (correlation 0.3); technical duplicates; three groups.
Size1,000 simulated data sets per scenario: 32,000 data sets in the main scenarios, each analysed by every design.
Added laterThree more designs, so that every combination of control set and rule for the number of factors that a user can select has its own measured result (Figure S10). And one scenario measured again: technical duplicates in transcriptomics and lipidomics/metabolomics, after a defect in the package was repaired.
Where it ranThe main scenarios on one workstation. The later runs and 21 of the 55 hard scenarios on rented cloud machines, after checking that both give the same results for the same simulated data. They did, with two things to report: a rounding-level difference in two secondary measures in 8 of 96,000 compared rows, which we accepted; and today's design, whose results differ slightly from machine to machine in some data sets (its averages stay close).
Real dataOne public data set per omic, 100 splits of one condition into two made-up groups (and 100 more lined up with the recorded batch in DIA and metabolomics), analysed with no RUV, today's design and the recommended design.
Designs comparedNo RUV; the true factor given to the model (the ceiling); today's design; and designs that change one choice at a time: the control set (all features, known true nulls, first-pass nulls, a contaminated list), the test form (factors in the model, or corrected data tested), the replicate structure (groups or technical replicates), and k (estimated or fixed at 1). Other published methods (RUV-4, RUVinv, surrogate variable analysis) were run for comparison.
MeasuresType I error rate (true nulls with p < 0.05); mean false discovery proportion among features called at q < 0.05; share of data sets with more than 10% false discoveries; power.
Decision rulesWritten down and independently reviewed before the confirming runs. A design passes when its type I error rate and mean false discovery proportion stay at or below 0.075 against a nominal 0.05 in every judged main scenario, and its power is not materially below the ceiling.
Extra-hard scenariosFourteen per simulator at 500 data sets each, thirteen in transcriptomics, where "50% missing" has no counterpart for counts (3 per group, 40% changed, strong confounding, three unwanted factors, 50% missing values, heavy-tailed noise and others, four of them combinations of two settings). They state where a design stops holding and were not used to choose between designs. All are complete in all four omics.

The decision rules

Seven rules, fixed and independently reviewed before the confirming runs. A design passes only if every rule holds in every main scenario; the limits allow for the uncertainty of 1,000 simulated data sets.

RuleWhat it asksLimitWhere the limit comes from
1Among features with no real difference, how many get p < 0.05 (and p < 0.01)?At most 0.075 (and 0.015)A robustness band from the statistics literature (Bradley 1978), taken from secondary sources and used on its upper side only
2On average, what share of the reported differences is false?At most 0.075The independent review; no literature source is stated
3How often does a single data set have more than 10% false discoveries?At most the best possible analysis's share plus 0.05The independent review: an average can hide a tail
4When unwanted variation exists, does the design gain over no RUV (more power, or fewer data sets above 10%)?A gain above 0The independent review
5When there is nothing to remove, does the design cost power?A loss of at most 0.02A stated judgement: the cost of fitting two factors that are not needed
6How often does the analysis crash?At most 0.01The independent review
7When no feature truly differs, how often is anything reported?At most 0.075The independent review
  • Limits apply to bounds, not to the measured values: one-sided 97.5% bounds, so a design passes only if it is inside the limit with that confidence. A design fails only if it is outside with the same confidence; in between it is "inconclusive".
  • Void scenarios. A scenario where even the best possible analysis misses rule 1 or 2 judges no design. Among the main scenarios this happened in the technical-duplicate scenario in transcriptomics and lipidomics/metabolomics (since repaired and measured again, Figure S2), and in the "no true differences" scenario in lipidomics/metabolomics.
  • Extra-hard scenarios never fail a design. They give its conditions of use.
  • One rule could not be met as written. Rule 4 asks for a gain, and in the scenario with no true differences there is nothing to gain, so every design fails it there, the best possible analysis included. Results are given both as written and with that scenario judged without rule 4.
  • What the rules gave for the recommended design. As written: no RUV design passes in any omic. With that one scenario judged without rule 4: pass in DIA, inconclusive in transcriptomics, and fail in classic proteomics and lipidomics/metabolomics, each time on rule 3 only (too many single data sets above 10%, although the average is inside the limit). The default is therefore our choice of the best-calibrated design measured, not a pass of the rules.
  • Designs with technical replicates were run in one scenario, so their "pass" rests on that scenario alone.

The rules exactly as declared, with their stated sources and every reading taken when they were applied, are in tableS-decision-rules.csv.

How to read the figures

  • Solid line: the nominal level, 0.05. Dashed line: the limit of the decision rule, 0.075. Below the dashed line is calibrated.
  • Ceiling: the analysis with the true unwanted factor handed to the model. No real method can do better, so a design that matches it has nothing left to gain.
  • One colour per design in every figure: green recommended (all features as controls), vermillion legacy, orange first-pass nulls, blue known null controls, grey no RUV, black ceiling. A lighter or darker shade is a variant of the same design.
  • Axes for false discoveries use a square-root scale so that values near 0.05 are not squashed. Numbers are written without the leading zero (.05 = 0.05).
  • Faded points and daggers (†) mark scenarios where even the ceiling failed, so no design was judged there.
  • § and filled squares mark the technical-duplicate scenario measured again after the package repair. ‡ marks hard scenarios that ran on cloud machines.

In both proteomics routes and in transcriptomics the recommended design stays close to 5% in almost every single data set, not only on average. In lipidomics/metabolomics the average is on target but single data sets vary widely: 15% to 28% of them exceed 10% false discoveries, and the best possible analysis varies too (8% to 26%).

An average can hide a tail, so this figure shows the spread over the 1,000 simulated data sets behind every average. Two things qualify the headline numbers. In classic proteomics with 12 samples per group, 12% of data sets exceed 10% under the recommended design against 1% for the best possible analysis. In lipidomics/metabolomics, with only 300 features and low power, few differences are reported per data set, which is the likely reason the proportion of false ones varies so much between data sets under every design, the best possible analysis included. Where today's design fails, its whole distribution sits high, not just a tail.

Figure S7. Number of unwanted factors: estimated against true

The automatic estimate returns the true number of unwanted factors in 93% to 100% of data sets that have one. The package's existing rule usually removes more factors than exist, and never returns zero. When there is no unwanted factor, the automatic estimate returns zero in 77% to 89% of data sets in three omics, but in only 36% in transcriptomics.

Figure S10. Does it matter how the number of factors is chosen?

Yes. With the same controls, the package's existing rule removes more factors than exist and never helps. The automatic estimate costs nothing with a supplied control list, and it repairs first-pass controls in lipidomics/metabolomics and transcriptomics, but not in DIA.

Two control sets let the user pick either rule, so each combination was measured. With a supplied control list, the existing rule costs little in the two proteomics routes, loses power in transcriptomics (61% against 76% of real differences found in the centre scenario) and loses both calibration and power in lipidomics/metabolomics (7.7% false discoveries against 5.0%; power 26% against 37%). With first-pass controls, switching to the automatic estimate brings lipidomics/metabolomics from 9.7% to 5.2% false discoveries in the centre scenario and restores the power lost in transcriptomics (77% against 60%). In DIA it changes almost nothing (9.0% against 9.4%): there the problem is the control set itself. First-pass controls still fail when the unwanted factor follows the groups, with either rule.

Final, all four omics. The recommended design never left a limit in classic proteomics, and in transcriptomics only where it reports nothing. It stops holding in DIA with 3 per group or three unwanted factors, and in lipidomics/metabolomics under strong confounding or a known unbalanced batch.

What was decided from this

Every design stays available in every omic, because users have legitimate reasons to prefer one (existing results to reproduce, a trusted control list, technical replicates). Each option carries its measured error rate and its conditions of use in the interface, the saved record and the report's methods text. The recommended design is pre-selected. It is our choice of the best-calibrated design measured; by the letter of the pre-set rules it did not pass in every omic (see the decision rules above).

The corrected data are still computed under every option and used for PCA, plots, export and multi-omics integration. "Factors in the model" changes only what the statistical test reads.

The number of unwanted factors is estimated automatically by a permutation method (the one used in surrogate variable analysis). This estimate is now pre-selected for every option where it can be computed, first-pass controls included, because the package's existing rule removed too many factors and never helped when both were tried on the same controls (Figure S10). The existing rule, which reads a diagnostic plot, stays selectable wherever a separate control set exists, with its measured result shown beside it, and the user can always set the number directly. Both rules come with a diagnostic plot.

Other decisions taken on the same day, none of them measured in this study: a feature missing in one whole group is still tested between the groups where it was measured, and flagged; in classic proteomics every protein of the normalised data is tested, and a protein without a corrected value is flagged; a warning is planned for when an estimated unwanted factor follows the groups; and four refinements of the statistics, found by audits of the code, will be measured in one more simulation round before they ship.

Limits, and what is still to come

  • The default is a judgement, not a pass of the rules. It did not pass every pre-set rule in every omic; it was the best-calibrated design measured, and its limits are those in the table near the top.
  • The real-data check is narrow. One public data set per omic, made-up groups, false calls only. It says nothing about power. It covered no RUV, today's design and the recommended design; the options with a supplied control list, first-pass controls and technical replicates were not checked on real data, nor were lipidomics, TMT, MaxQuant LFQ and the classic DIA route.
  • The simulations may be harsher than real data. In the one real data set where we measured it, the batch effect was a quarter to a third as strong as the simulated unwanted factor, and not correcting was adequate there although the simulation said otherwise.
  • Technical replicates rest on thin evidence. That option was judged in one scenario in each proteomics route. In transcriptomics and lipidomics/metabolomics the scenario was void, then repaired and measured again once, on a cloud machine, where it behaved well; it does not count for or against a design.
  • Some conditions could not be tested at all, because even the best possible analysis failed: lipidomics/metabolomics with 3 per group and with no true differences; heavy-tailed noise and one-directional effects in transcriptomics; 3 per group with three factors in classic proteomics.
  • DIA was measured on one of the two quantification engines (the fast reimplementation). The result is expected, not measured, to carry over to the reference limpa engine. On data with no missing values the DIA route failed to run in half of the simulated data sets.
  • Two machines. Part of the results ran on cloud machines. All designs gave matching results on both except today's design, whose results vary slightly from machine to machine; one rounding-level difference in two secondary measures (8 of 96,000 rows) was accepted and is reported.
  • An on-target average is not an on-target data set in lipidomics/metabolomics. With 300 features and low power, 15% to 28% of single data sets exceed 10% false discoveries under the recommended design, and 8% to 26% under the best possible analysis (Figure S6).
  • The automatic estimate of the number of factors over-counts in transcriptomics when there is nothing to remove (zero returned in 36% of such data sets, Figure S7). The error rate stayed on target there and power fell by less than one point.
  • Still to come: one more simulation round for four refinements of the statistics before release (the numbers on this page are for the method as simulated); the threshold of the planned warning; hard scenarios for the three designs added later.
